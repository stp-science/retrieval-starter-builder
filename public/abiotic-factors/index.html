Investigating habitats

What shapes a habitat?

A paddock and a forest can be close together but offer very different conditions for life. Measuring their non-living features helps us investigate why different organisms grow there.

Your research challenge

Read each field note and complete your worksheet. Identify the equipment, turn the explanation into three method steps, find the unit or scale, and explain one mistake to avoid.

Use short notes in your own words. Be ready to explain why your chosen mistake would change the result.

FIELD NOTE 01
Light reaching a surface

Light intensity

Leaves need light for photosynthesis. A canopy can intercept sunlight before it reaches plants on the forest floor. To investigate this, ecologists use a light meter with a sensor that detects incoming light.

At the sampling point, uncover the sensor and hold it facing upwards. Keep its height and orientation consistent between sites. Once the display is steady, write down the value in lux (lx), checking whether your meter requires a range multiplier. A shadow cast by a person can make the reading describe the person’s shade rather than the habitat.

Think it through: Two students sample the same patch. One holds the sensor beside their leg, while the other holds it away from their body. Why might their readings disagree?

FIELD NOTE 02
Particles in the soil

Soil texture

Soil contains mineral particles of different sizes: sand, silt and clay. Their mixture affects how water moves through the soil. Texture describes these particles; it does not simply mean whether the soil is wet or dry.

A simple field test uses a trowel to collect soil from just below the leaf litter. Remove obvious stones and roots. Add a little water if needed, then rub the soil between your fingers and try rolling it. Grit suggests sand, a smooth or floury feel suggests silt, and a sticky, mouldable feel suggests clay. Use a soil texture identification chart to judge the mixture. Many soils contain all three, so one sensation alone cannot identify an exact percentage.

The result is a description or category, such as sandy soil, clay soil or loam, rather than a measurement in grams. Sample the same depth in both habitats: comparing forest topsoil with paddock subsoil would introduce another difference. Wash your hands after handling soil.

Think it through: Does a wet sample automatically contain more clay? Explain what you would need to examine before deciding.

FIELD NOTE 03
Below the surface

Soil temperature

Conditions around roots and soil organisms can differ from those in the air above. A soil thermometer or suitable temperature probe measures this in degrees Celsius (°C).

Brush aside loose leaf litter and gently insert the probe about 5 cm into the soil. Do not force it against stones or hard ground. Allow about a minute, and longer if the reading is still changing, before recording the value. Read an analogue dial face-on. Use the same depth each time and clean the probe between samples.

Think it through: A probe has just come from a warm classroom. Why might its first reading in cool soil be misleading?

FIELD NOTE 04
Our classroom method

Soil pH

Different plants grow best under different chemical conditions. A pH test tells us how acidic or alkaline a sample is. The classroom scale usually runs from 0 to 14: values below 7 are acidic, 7 is neutral, and values above 7 are alkaline. pH has no units.

For this investigation, label a beaker with the collection location and use a balance to measure 20 g of soil into it. Measure 40 mL of distilled water with a measuring cylinder and add it to the soil. Distilled water helps us avoid introducing substances found in tap water. Stir with a rod for about one minute, then leave the mixture for 2–3 minutes.

Pour the mixture through filter paper supported in a funnel, collecting the liquid in a clean container. Dip a fresh strip of pH paper into this liquid for the time stated on its packaging. Remove the strip and compare it with the supplied colour chart at the recommended reading time. Record your judgement; ask a partner to check if the colour falls between two values.

Keep the amount of soil and water consistent across samples. Reusing dirty equipment can carry material from one sample into the next. Take three readings using fresh strips and record all of them. Follow your teacher’s instructions for summarising the results and disposing of the soil.

FIELD NOTE 05
Above the ground

Air temperature

Air temperature influences the conditions experienced by organisms above the soil. It is measured with a thermometer or temperature probe, with readings recorded in degrees Celsius (°C).

Choose a consistent height, such as one metre above the ground, and keep the sensor away from your skin and breath. Shield it from direct sunlight without enclosing it or preventing air movement. Let the reading settle before recording it. Otherwise, the instrument may still be adjusting to where it was stored, or may be warmed directly by sunlight.

Think it through: A thermometer resting on a sunlit rock gives a higher reading than one held in the air. Are both measuring air temperature in the same way?

Bring the evidence together

A fair comparison needs a plan.

Your worksheet asks you to compare a paddock and a forest. Choose two things to keep the same and explain how each would help. Then consider whether one reading could represent a whole habitat.

Need a starting point?

Think about when you sample, exactly where you place an instrument, and how you use it. Keep your measuring procedure consistent while allowing the habitats themselves to differ.
